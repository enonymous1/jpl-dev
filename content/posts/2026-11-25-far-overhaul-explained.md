The Federal Acquisition Regulation is being rewritten for the first time, comprehensively, in its history. If you work anywhere near a federal offer — writing one, pricing one, or evaluating one — you have probably heard about it, and you have probably also heard three different versions of what it means. Most of the confusion comes from one mistake: treating everything labeled "overhaul" as if it were already the law.

This is a practitioner's read, not legal advice. I worked GSA Professional Services Refresh 31 pricing and proposal support in early 2026, so I come at this from the side of the offer — the pricing workbook and the proposal — not from the contracting officer's side. Statuses and dates below were checked against Acquisition.gov and the Federal Register on November 25, 2026. This is a moving target: a final rule landing would change the picture, and I will update this post when one does.

## TL;DR

- **What it is:** Executive Order 14275 (April 2025) ordered the first comprehensive rewrite of the FAR — back to its statutory roots, in plain language, with most non-statutory rules removed. OFPP and the FAR Council run it.
- **What is actually in force:** model deviation texts for 51 FAR parts, adopted agency by agency. Those deviations are what agencies are applying right now.
- **What is not in force:** the proposed rules. Eight of twelve planned proposed rules are published, covering 36 parts. Comment periods have closed. **Zero final rules exist yet.**
- **What to do:** find the deviation for the part that governs your work, read the solicitation's own terms, and use the FAR Companion for the practical "how" — it is guidance, not a rule.
- **The GSA angle:** GSA is running its own GSAR overhaul in parallel, and that is the track that touches a MAS offer most directly.

## What the overhaul is

The FAR is the rulebook executive agencies follow when they buy goods and services. It has grown for decades by accretion: statute, regulation, policy, and habit layered on top of each other until the rulebook itself became part of the cost of doing business with the government.

Executive Order 14275, *Restoring Common Sense to Federal Procurement*, signed April 15, 2025, directs a rewrite with three stated aims: return the FAR to its statutory roots, rewrite it in plain language, and remove most non-statutory rules. The work is led by the Office of Federal Procurement Policy (OFPP) with the FAR Council. The practical buying guidance that used to live inside the FAR is moving out into non-regulatory buying guides — most visibly the FAR Companion — which tell buyers how to buy without carrying the force of a regulation.

The stated goal is faster acquisitions, greater competition, and better results. Whether it delivers that is an empirical question that will take years to answer. What matters today is narrower: what has actually changed, and what has only been proposed.

## The three stages, and why they get confused

Everything about this overhaul exists in one of three stages, and the stages do not have the same force. This is the distinction the whole post turns on.

| Stage | What it is | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Model deviation** | OFPP's rewritten text for a FAR part, issued as a model for agencies to adopt | Agencies adopt these through their own class deviations. As adopted, this is the operative text — the rules agencies are actually applying right now. |
| **Proposed rule** | A formal rulemaking notice in the Federal Register | It proposes changing the codified FAR. It does not change it. Until a final rule takes effect, the codified FAR plus any deviation in the contract is what binds. |
| **Final rule** | A rulemaking that has gone through comment and been published as final | This is the only stage that changes the FAR itself. None exist yet in this overhaul. |

A proposed rule does not change the codified FAR. A model deviation does not either, on its own — but once an agency adopts it as a class deviation, that agency is buying under the rewritten text. So the honest answer to "has the FAR changed?" is: the text agencies are using has changed in most parts; the codified FAR has not changed at all.

## Where things stand

| Piece | Stage | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Executive Order 14275 | In effect | Signed April 15, 2025 |
| Model deviations, 51 FAR parts | Issued; adopted agency by agency | First issued May 2, 2025; 12 updated since, latest Part 9 on Nov 17, 2026 |
| Proposed rules, 8 of 12 planned | Published; comment periods closed | Four on June 23, 2026; four on Sept 18, 2026; comments closed July 23 / Oct 19, 2026 |
| Final rules | None | Zero published as of Nov 25, 2026 |
| FAR Companion, version 3.0 | Guidance, not a rule | Posted Oct 30, 2026 |
| Part 19 (small business) rewrite | Interim class deviation, in practical use | Released Sept 26, 2025; not yet through formal rulemaking |
| SAM.gov modernized representations and certifications | Live and in effect | March 24, 2026 |
| GSAR overhaul | Parallel track; proposed rule comments closed | GSAR Case 2026-G502 comment period ended Nov 4, 2026 |

Two details in that table deserve emphasis. First, the eight published proposed rules cover 36 parts, which means 15 parts have no proposed rule yet — including Part 19 (small business), Part 31 (cost principles), Part 32 (contract financing), and Parts 42 and 43 (contract administration and modifications). Second, the only concrete, already-effective change inside the overhaul that touches every vendor is the SAM.gov representations and certifications update — the rest is still deviation or proposal.

Two of the published proposals show what the rewrite actually does when it reaches a part. Case 2026-003 would change source selection: it would formally recognize award to the highest technically rated offeror at a fair and reasonable price, let contracting officers accept late proposals when it is in the Government's interest and would not unduly delay award, and remove the commercial omnibus provision and clause at FAR 52.212-3 and 52.212-5. Case 2026-011 would rewrite data rights: it would delete subpart 27.4, Rights in Data and Copyrights, and replace it with DFARS-derived coverage, including a new subpart 27.5 for commercial technical data and software. Both are proposals. Neither is final.

## What it changes for someone pricing a GSA offer

This is the part I have a view on, because it is the work I did: GSA Professional Services Refresh 31 pricing and proposal support. A refresh is where a MAS holder updates its offer — labor categories, pricing, terms — and the FAR is the background law that the whole workbook sits on. Here is what the overhaul does and does not change for that work, as of today.

- **The pricing rules have not moved.** Cost principles live in FAR Part 31, and Part 31 is one of the 15 parts with no proposed rule yet. Whatever the overhaul eventually does to cost principles, the rules you price under today are the rules you priced under last year.
- **The text you cite may be deviation text.** If a solicitation or a refresh instruction cites a rewritten part, the operative language is the agency's adopted deviation, not the codified FAR. Cite the deviation. If you quote the old FAR text in a proposal and the evaluator is working from the deviation, you are answering a different rulebook.
- **The Companion is now where the "how" lives.** The FAR Companion is non-regulatory guidance, but it is the document that tells a buyer — and, by extension, an offeror reading the buyer's mind — how the rewritten rules are meant to be applied in practice. Read it the way you used to read the FAR's own explanatory text.
- **GSAR is the track that touches a MAS offer most directly.** The FAR overhaul is government-wide; the GSAR overhaul is GSA's own regulation, and it governs the schedule. A MAS holder should be watching the GSAR track at least as closely as the FAR track.
- **Plain language cuts both ways.** Shorter, clearer rules are easier to comply with — and easier for an evaluator to check you against. A rewrite that removes ambiguity also removes the gray area that used to absorb a sloppy citation.

None of this is a reason to stop work or wait for final rules. The deviations are the working text now, and offers are being evaluated under them. Waiting for the codified FAR to catch up means waiting an indefinite time.

## What to do now

| Step | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Find the deviation | For each FAR part that governs your work, pull the model deviation text from Acquisition.gov and check whether your agency adopted it | The deviation is the text being applied, not the codified FAR |
| 2. Read the solicitation | The solicitation's own terms and clauses govern your offer | A deviation in the background does not override what the solicitation actually says |
| 3. Use the Companion | Read the FAR Companion for the parts you work under | It is the practical guidance for the rewritten rules |
| 4. Watch GSAR | Track the GSAR overhaul alongside the FAR track | It governs the schedule directly |
| 5. Do not cite a proposal as final | A proposed rule is not the law, and a comment period closing is not a final rule | Citing one as final in a proposal is a credibility problem with the evaluator |

## What I am watching

- **The remaining four proposed rules.** Twelve were planned; eight are published. The unpublished four include the proposals for Part 19 and the other parts still waiting.
- **The first final rule.** The moment one publishes, the "nothing is final" framing in this post goes stale, and I will update it.
- **Part 19's formal rulemaking.** The small-business rewrite is already operating as an interim deviation; its formal rule will be the test of how much of the deviation text survives comment.
- **The GSAR track.** For anyone holding a MAS contract, the GSAR overhaul is where the changes land first.
- **Whether the plain-language rewrite survives contact with practice.** The FAR Council's Part 1 proposal would require the FAR to be reviewed, with public input, at least every four years. A rewrite that stays rewritten is a different outcome from a rewrite that accretes again.
